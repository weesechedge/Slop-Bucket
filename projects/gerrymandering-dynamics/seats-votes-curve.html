Seats–votes model · bias + responsiveness

Two dials, one fair setting.

Each axis is a party's own share. The dashed diagonal is proportionality (seats = votes). Responsiveness (ρ) bends the curve's steepness; bias (β) slides Blue and Red apart. The map is symmetric exactly when the two curves coincide — and disproportionality is just the curve's distance from the diagonal, which responsiveness alone controls.

What each move shows. Set ρ = 1 and the curves lie on the diagonal — perfectly proportional, zero seat bonus. Push ρ up and the S-curve steepens: small leads convert to large majorities (responsive), and the shaded gap from the diagonal — the disproportionality — grows with it. They are the same fact. Now move β off zero: Blue and Red separate, and the vertical gap between them at the 50% line is the bias — the seats one party gets that the other wouldn't at the identical vote share. Fairness-as-symmetry is the single demand β = 0; it says nothing about ρ. Choosing ρ — proportional mirror, decisive majority-maker, or near winner-take-all — is the open value question a fair process still has to answer.
